Compounded Peptides Explained

In October 2026, HHS told pharmacies it would stop enforcing the rules against compounding six popular peptides. Here is what that really means, what it doesn't, and what the science says about each one.

Policy ExplainerPeptidesPatient Safety
!
Not approved. Just not enforced.

No peptide was approved by FDA, and none was added to the official list of ingredients pharmacies may use. HHS said it won't take action against pharmacies that meet ten strict conditions while it writes formal rules. The policy can be changed or withdrawn at any time.1,2

The simple version

Think of a speed limit sign

The rule is still there

Legally, these six peptides still can't be compounded. The law itself didn't change.

The police said they won't ticket

HHS announced it won't enforce that rule, but only for pharmacies that meet every condition.

It can change overnight

HHS can revisit it if safety problems appear, and a future administration can withdraw it.

Why it was needed

Three gates, all closed

A compounding pharmacy (a "503A" pharmacy, which makes a medicine for one patient's prescription) can only use a raw ingredient that passes through one of three gates. None of the six peptides gets through any of them.1

The workaround: "enforcement discretion." Instead of opening a gate, HHS said it won't stop pharmacies from going around them while FDA writes a rule to add the peptides to the Bulks List, which is expected in 2027.

How we got here

The timeline

2023

FDA flags peptides as risky

BPC-157 and many other popular peptides go into "Category 2," FDA's list of bulk ingredients with significant safety concerns.3

April 2026

Removed from Category 2

FDA takes the peptides off the safety-concern list. That lowered the warning, but it did not make them legal to compound.1

July 23–24, 2026

Advisory committee votes yes on 6 of 7

FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee narrowly recommends six peptides. FDA's own scientists had recommended against all seven, citing too little clinical data.4,5

October 28, 2026

HHS signs the enforcement policy

Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. signs it from the HHS Secretary's office, not FDA. It's published in the Federal Register on October 30.1,2

November 2026

Comments and pushback

Public comments close Nov 13. The Endocrine Society writes to oppose it, and a patient-safety group files a petition against it.6,7

2027 (expected)

Formal rulemaking

FDA is expected to propose adding the peptides to the Bulks List. A second committee meeting on more peptides is reportedly planned.

The fine print

The 10 conditions pharmacies must meet

A pharmacy is only protected if it meets every one. The ones marked in amber are the hardest for many pharmacies to meet.1,2

1

Covered peptide

It must be one of the six on HHS's Interim Access List.

2

Exact identity and route

It must match HHS's specified molecular structure and use only the approved routes for that peptide.

3

Tested, registered source Hard

The ingredient comes from an FDA-registered facility with a Certificate of Analysis for purity, potency, and contaminants.

4

Real manufacturer verified Hard

The original maker must be FDA-registered and meet good manufacturing practice (cGMP). Most overseas "research use only" supply fails this.

5

Report problems

Serious side effects and quality issues go to FDA MedWatch and to the states involved.

6

Clear label

The label must say "This is a compounded drug product. Not for resale."

7

One peptide per product No blends

Combinations such as BPC-157 + TB-500 in one vial aren't covered.

8

Oral forms need proof Hard

Pills or liquids need documentation that the peptide is actually absorbed in humans.

9

Approved syringes only

The only delivery device allowed is an FDA-cleared syringe. No pens or novel devices.

10

All other rules still apply

Sanitary conditions, a patient-specific prescription, and your state's pharmacy laws.

Who it affects

What it means for you

Pharmacies

  • A narrow openingProtection only if every condition is met.
  • Sourcing is the bottleneckCompliant, cGMP ingredient supply will be tight at first.
  • States can be stricterYour board of pharmacy still has the final say.
  • Plan for changeThe policy is temporary and can be withdrawn.

Prescribers

  • Still unapproved drugsMost of the evidence is from animals.
  • Standard of care appliesMedical boards and malpractice carriers still judge you on it.
  • Real patient relationshipA patient-specific prescription and informed consent are still required.

Patients

  • Safer than the gray marketBetter than unlabeled "research" vials bought online.
  • Compoundable doesn't mean provenHuman evidence is thin for most of these peptides.
  • Expect cash payA prescription is required, and insurance won't cover it.
  • Athletes bewareSeveral are banned in tested sports, even with a prescription.

The six peptides

What each one is, and what the science says

About the doses below: none of these peptides has an FDA-approved dose. The amounts shown are protocols commonly reported in clinical practice, mostly estimated from animal studies. Controlled human trials haven't confirmed them. They're here for education only. Only a licensed prescriber who knows your health history should decide whether any of these is right for you, and at what dose.
BPC

BPC-157

"Body Protection Compound." 15 amino acids, based on a protein in stomach juice.
Under the skinMuscleOral*Topical
Human evidence
Small pilot studies only
How it may work

Encourages new blood vessel growth and blood flow to injured tissue, and may protect the lining of the gut.

Commonly used for
  • Tendon, ligament, and muscle injuries
  • Joint pain and recovery after surgery
  • Gut lining problems, ulcers, and IBD
Commonly reported protocol

250–500 mcg under the skin, 1–2× daily, for 4–8 weeks

For gut problems, 250–500 mcg by mouth daily.

The evidence

Many rodent studies. In humans, only tiny pilot and case reports, with no randomized trials.

Possible benefits

Faster soft-tissue healing and gut protection in animals. Human benefit is unproven.

Cautions

Because it grows blood vessels, it raises a theoretical concern with active or past cancer. Long-term safety is unknown. Banned in tested sports.

TB

TB-500

A lab-made piece of thymosin beta-4, a natural repair protein.
Under the skinMuscle
Human evidence
None for the fragment itself
How it may work

Helps cells move into damaged areas, and supports new blood vessels and tissue repair with less scarring.

Commonly used for
  • Muscle and soft-tissue injuries
  • Wound healing and recovery
  • Flexibility and mobility
Commonly reported protocol

Loading: 2–2.5 mg under the skin, 2× weekly for 4–6 weeks

Then a maintenance dose of about 2 mg every 1–2 weeks.

The evidence

Animal and lab studies. The human trials used the full thymosin beta-4 protein (for example in eye drops), not TB-500.

Possible benefits

Tissue repair and less inflammation in animals. Human benefit is unproven.

Cautions

The same theoretical cancer concern from blood vessel growth. Banned in tested sports. It can't be mixed with BPC-157 under this policy.

EPI

Epitalon

A 4-amino-acid peptide developed in Russia, modeled on a pineal gland extract.
Under the skinMuscleShort courses
Human evidence
Older, small Russian studies
How it may work

May switch on telomerase, which protects the ends of chromosomes (seen in lab cells), and may help regulate melatonin and sleep.

Commonly used for
  • Healthy aging and "longevity"
  • Sleep and body-clock support
Commonly reported protocol

5–10 mg under the skin daily for 10–20 days

Repeated 1–2 times a year. The policy requires it to be given in courses like this, not continuously.

The evidence

Lab and animal work, plus older Russian studies in older adults with real design limits. No modern randomized trials.

Possible benefits

Antioxidant and sleep effects, and longevity signals in animals. Human benefit is unproven.

Cautions

Switching on telomerase raises a theoretical cancer concern. Long-term human data is minimal.

SMX

Semax

A modified fragment of the hormone ACTH. An approved drug in Russia.
Nasal only
Human evidence
Russian clinical use, little Western data
How it may work

Raises BDNF, a brain growth factor, and affects dopamine and serotonin. It may protect brain cells.

Commonly used for
  • Focus, memory, and mental fatigue
  • Recovery after stroke or concussion
  • Mood support
Commonly reported protocol

About 200–900 mcg a day as nose drops or spray

Split into 2–3 doses, often in 2–4 week cycles. Russian stroke protocols use higher doses.

The evidence

The most human data of the six, but it's mostly published in Russian and has rarely been repeated in the West.

Possible benefits

Better attention and stroke recovery in Russian studies.

Cautions

Nasal irritation, headache, and possible anxiety or overstimulation. Use care with mental health conditions.

KPV

KPV

3 amino acids: the anti-inflammatory tail of the hormone alpha-MSH.
Oral*Topical
Human evidence
No published human trials
How it may work

Calms the NF-κB inflammation pathway inside gut and skin cells, without the skin-darkening effect of full alpha-MSH.

Commonly used for
  • Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's
  • Gut inflammation
  • Eczema, psoriasis, and wound healing (topical)
Commonly reported protocol

About 200–500 mcg by mouth daily

Topical creams, applied 1–2× daily. Strengths vary.

The evidence

Lab cells and mouse colitis models only.

Possible benefits

Less gut and skin inflammation in animals. Human benefit is unproven.

Cautions

Human safety data is minimal. Never use it in place of proven IBD treatment.

MOTS

MOTS-c Weight-loss relevant

16 amino acids, made from instructions in your mitochondria's own DNA.
Under the skinMuscle
Human evidence
Early trial of a related drug
How it may work

Switches on AMPK, the cell's energy sensor, which helps muscles take up sugar and respond to insulin. It's often called an "exercise mimic."

Commonly used for
  • Insulin resistance and blood sugar
  • Weight management
  • Endurance and healthy aging
Commonly reported protocol

5–10 mg under the skin, 1–3× weekly

Often 5 mg three times a week for 4–6 weeks, then a check-in with your prescriber.

The evidence

Strong mouse data on obesity and insulin resistance. An early human trial of a related drug showed only modest signals.

Possible benefits

Better insulin sensitivity and less weight gain in animals. Human benefit is unproven.

Cautions

Risk of low blood sugar when combined with a GLP-1, insulin, or sulfonylureas. It's not a substitute for proven GLP-1 therapy.

* Oral forms are covered only when the pharmacy can document that the peptide is absorbed in humans.

The debate

✓ The case for it

  • Harm reduction: people are already injecting unregulated vials bought online
  • Patient choice and freedom
  • Years of real-world use
  • Pulls demand away from foreign "research use only" suppliers

HHS, many integrative clinicians, and free-market groups

× The case against it

  • It overrides FDA's own scientists
  • Human safety data is thin or missing
  • It sets a precedent of political appointees bypassing FDA
  • "Compounded" may look like "proven" to patients

Endocrine Society, patient-safety groups, and policy analysts6,7

The bottom line

  1. Nothing was approved. HHS simply isn't enforcing the rules for six peptides while formal rules are written.
  2. Pharmacies have to meet all 10 conditions: one peptide per product, a verified FDA-registered source, approved forms only, and side-effect reporting.
  3. Pharmacy-made beats gray-market vials, but the science is still early for every one of these peptides.
  4. Watch for the 2027 rulemaking, state board decisions, and legal challenges.

Current as of November 21, 2026. Policies are changing quickly.
