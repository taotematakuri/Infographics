Evidence Review · Book Decode No. 02

The Hunger Code, Decoded

An evidence-balanced field guide to Jason Fung's follow-up to The Obesity Code. The question shifts from "how much you eat" to "how hungry you are, and why." Here is what the book gets right about ultra-processed food, appetite, and GLP-1s, and where the science still pushes back.

Book: The Hunger Code (2026) Author: Jason Fung, MD Framework: Three hungers Program: Three Golden Rules Our stance: Evidence-balanced
The one-sentence thesis

The real problem was never how many calories we eat. It was always how hungry we are, and why. Lower the hunger, and the calories take care of themselves.

The sequel's move: The Obesity Code asked what and when to eat. The Hunger Code asks why we eat at all.
01 · The reframe

Stop fighting hunger. Decode it.

The Obesity Code put insulin at the center of fat storage. The Hunger Code steps back one link and asks what drives eating in the first place. Willpower loses to hunger every time, so the goal is to lower the hunger, not to out-muscle it.

Same destination, different route. Both models want you eating less. Fung's argument is that you get there by lowering the drive to eat, not by resisting it, which is why the book is organized around the sources of hunger rather than a calorie target.

02 · The spine of the program

The Three Golden Rules

Fung distills the entire book into three rules. Everything else, the three hungers, the fat thermostat, and all fifty practical tips, exists to serve these three. If a member remembers nothing else, they should remember this.

1

Avoid ultra-processed foods

Remove the engineered, hyper-palatable products designed to override fullness. This is the single highest-confidence move in the book.

2

Don't eat all the time. Fast regularly.

Constant eating means insulin and hunger signals never fully reset. Deliberate gaps between meals, and regular fasting, give the body time to switch from storing to burning.

3

Commit to a health mindset and healthy social habits

Lasting change is social and psychological, not just dietary. Accountability, environment, and the people around you decide whether the first two rules stick.

Read the rest of this guide as the "how" behind these three: rule one drives the ultra-processed-food section, rule two runs through timing and fasting, and rule three underpins the mindset and social-hunger material.

03 · The organizing idea

Three hungers, three fixes

The book's backbone: not all hunger is the same, so not all hunger has the same cure. Each type carries a scientific name and an everyday one. Name which is driving you, and the right response follows.

Hunger 1 · Physical / Homeostatic

Physical hunger

The true biological signal, run by hormones like ghrelin and leptin. When you diet, the body fights back by raising this hunger and slowing metabolism.

Fix: real food, protein, and fiber, with stable insulin, so the signal settles instead of spiking.

Hunger 2 · Emotional / Hedonic

Emotional (reward) hunger

Eating for pleasure, comfort, or stress relief rather than need. Ultra-processed foods are engineered to trigger the brain's reward system on demand.

Fix: remove the cues and the hyper-palatable foods rather than relying on moderation in the moment.

Hunger 3 · Social / Conditioned

Social (habit) hunger

Eating because it is noon, because everyone else is, because the movie is on. Driven by routine, environment, and social occasion, not appetite.

Fix: change the pattern, the timing, and the social defaults; break the cue-to-eat loop.

The diagnostic question the whole book turns on: before you eat, ask which hunger this is. Physical hunger deserves food. Emotional and social hunger deserve a different answer.

04 · The fat thermostat

Your brain sets a target, and defends it

Carried over from The Obesity Code and made central here: the hypothalamus behaves like a thermostat for body fat, defending a target level. Diets that only cut calories trip the defense, more hunger, slower metabolism. Resetting the target, Fung argues, means keeping insulin low and hunger quiet over time, not white-knuckling a deficit.

Why it reframes maintenance. If the thermostat is defended, weight regain is the body doing its job, not the person failing. Whether insulin is truly the master control is where the evidence gets argued (section 09).

05 · The mechanism

A calorie is not a calorie to your hormones

Fung's core mechanistic claim: identical calories can send different hormonal messages depending on the food. One says "store fat." The other says "you're full." This is the hinge the practical program hangs on.

06 · Golden Rule 1 in depth

Ultra-processed food, engineered to override fullness

The villain of the title, and the first Golden Rule. Ultra-processed foods are designed to digest fast, hit the fat-sugar-salt "bliss point," and slip past the body's satiety signals. Fung draws an explicit parallel to the tobacco industry's playbook.

07 · The GLP-1 argument

Why Ozempic proves his point

Fung turns the GLP-1 boom into evidence for the whole thesis. People on semaglutide or tirzepatide eat roughly a thousand fewer calories a day, and not because their willpower improved overnight. The drugs quiet hunger by mimicking satiety hormones. If reducing hunger is what makes the medications work, then hunger, not calorie arithmetic, was the lever all along.

The clinical bridge for your work: the medication and the method aim at the same target. GLP-1s lower hunger pharmacologically; the book's food, timing, and social changes lower it behaviorally. Used together, they complement rather than compete.

09 · Where the wider evidence lands

What holds up, what's overstated, what's still argued

The evidence-balanced core. The Hunger Code is on its strongest ground when it talks about ultra-processed food, appetite, timing, sleep, and satiety. It is on its most contested ground when it makes insulin the sole explanation and treats total calories as beside the point. All three columns are usable if you stay honest about which is which.

Well supported

  • Ultra-processed food drives overeating. A growing consensus, backed by controlled feeding work showing people eat more on UPF diets.
  • Hunger, not willpower, governs intake. GLP-1 drugs work precisely by lowering appetite, which supports the book's framing.
  • Food order and meal timing matter. Eating carbs last and earlier does blunt glucose and insulin responses.
  • Sleep and stress shape hunger hormones. Short sleep reliably raises ghrelin and appetite.
  • Protein and fiber increase satiety, so whole foods genuinely make you fuller per calorie.

Overstated / contested

  • Insulin as the sole master switch. Metabolic-ward studies show calorie reduction produces fat loss regardless of insulin levels.
  • Total calories treated as beside the point. Energy balance is still the ultimate ledger; hunger and hormones decide how easily you stay within it.
  • Food addiction as settled fact. A useful lens, but the criteria and prevalence estimates are still debated.
  • Abstinence over moderation. Intuitive, but supported by limited clinical-trial data so far.

Still being argued

  • Is fasting superior, or just another way to eat less? A 2023 RCT found alternate-day fasting no better than plain calorie reduction.
  • How big are the timing effects over months, once total intake is held constant?
  • Individual variation. The insulin-resistant may respond differently than the insulin-sensitive; the book generalizes.
  • Long-term sustainability of strict abstinence and fasting remains thin in the research.
The honest synthesis. The Hunger Code is most persuasive where it overlaps with mainstream nutrition science: ultra-processed foods promote overconsumption, appetite is physiological rather than a character flaw, and food quality, timing, sleep, and protein all shape how full you feel. It overreaches when it elevates insulin to the sole cause and treats total calories as irrelevant; calories remain the ultimate energy ledger, even if hunger is what determines them. For coaching and content, plant your flag on the high-confidence overlap: eat mostly whole food, cut the ultra-processed stuff, use food order and timing, protect sleep, build supportive social habits, and treat hunger as information rather than an enemy.
10 · Myth vs fact

The careful version, ready for a segment

How the book gets flattened into slogans, and what it more responsibly says.

Common claim

"The Hunger Code says calories don't count."

More accurate

Calories still balance the books and remain the ultimate energy ledger. The point is that hunger decides how many you eat, so lowering hunger is the practical lever, not sheer restraint.

Common claim

"Ultra-processed food is bad only because it's high in calories."

More accurate

The problem is how it is engineered: fast digestion, low fiber and protein, and a bliss point that overrides fullness, so you eat more before you feel satisfied.

Common claim

"Ozempic proves you can ignore diet entirely."

More accurate

GLP-1s prove hunger is the lever. They lower appetite; the same food and habits do too. The book pairs them rather than replacing one with the other.

Common claim

"Just eat your carbs last and the weight falls off."

More accurate

Food order genuinely blunts insulin spikes, but it is one small lever among many. Real change comes from the whole pattern and the three Golden Rules, not a single trick.

11 · Talking points, part one

For the weight-loss & health courses

Framed for teaching members through GLP-1 Foundations, Restart, and the Wellness Transformation arc. Education and lifestyle coaching, with medical decisions left to each member's own prescriber.

Teach hunger as a skill

Anchor every lesson to the three Golden Rules
01

Lead with the three Golden Rules

Give members the whole program in one breath: avoid ultra-processed food, don't eat all the time, and commit to a health mindset and healthy social habits. Everything else is the "how."

02

Open meals with the three-hunger check

Teach one habit that reframes everything. Say: "Is this physical, emotional, or social hunger? Only the physical kind actually needs food."

03

Match the fix to the hunger

Physical hunger wants real food and protein. Emotional hunger wants the trigger removed. Social hunger wants the pattern and the social defaults changed. It stops members from throwing willpower at every craving.

04

Make ultra-processed food the shared enemy

Shift blame from the member to the food environment. The design of UPFs, not personal weakness, is why they overeat. This lands as relief and it is the book's best-supported claim.

05

Hand them the quick levers

Carbs last, eat earlier, add vinegar, whole fruit not juice, protect sleep, eat to 80% full. Small, teachable, and each one lowers hunger or insulin a little.

06

Position it as the GLP-1 companion

For medicated members, this is the "what do I actually eat and do" layer. The drug lowers hunger; these habits lower it too and carry the results after the dose changes.

07

Stay on defensible ground

Coach the overlap: whole food, less ultra-processed, food order, timing, sleep, and social support. Skip "calories don't count" and hard claims about food addiction. Credibility is the brand's asset.

12 · Talking points, part two

For the podcast

Angles, hooks, and discussion beats for a Healthier Ever After episode on The Hunger Code, built for conversation between you and Greg with room for the evidence-balanced turn your listeners trust.

Hook, tension, honest landing

A full episode arc in ten beats
01

Cold-open hook

"What if the problem was never how much you eat, but how hungry you are, and why?" That reframe is the most shareable line in the book.

02

The sequel angle

Frame it against The Obesity Code, which you already covered. That book asked what and when; this one asks why. A natural two-parter.

03

The three Golden Rules

Give listeners the whole program up front, then spend the episode unpacking each rule. It gives the conversation a spine and something to take away.

04

The three hungers, live

Walk through physical, emotional, and social hunger with real examples. Have each of you confess which one trips you up. It is instantly relatable audio.

05

The cookie vs eggs moment

Same calories, opposite hormonal messages. It is the vivid, quotable image that makes "a calorie isn't a calorie" click for a listener.

06

The Big Food villain

The tobacco parallel and the roughly 58% versus 18% ultra-processed contrast make a strong, righteous segment. Keep it about the food design, not shaming eaters.

07

The Ozempic tie-in

Your wheelhouse. Explain how GLP-1 drugs quietly prove the hunger thesis, then bridge to your medication episodes and courses.

08

The honest pushback and steelman

The turn your audience expects: insulin is not the whole story and total calories still matter, said kindly. Then be fair: the practical advice is sound and the UPF case is strong.

09

Listener discussion questions

"Which of the three hungers runs your evenings? What's the one ultra-processed food you can't keep in the house?" Invite replies for a follow-up.

10

The one-line takeaway

"Eat real food, ditch the engineered stuff, give your body real breaks from eating, and treat hunger as information instead of an enemy." That is the book at its best, and it holds up.
