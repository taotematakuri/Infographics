Stress and Cortisol Made Simple

Cortisol is your main stress hormone. In short bursts it helps: it wakes you up and gives you energy to handle a hard moment. Trouble starts when stress never lets up, and your sleep, cravings, and mood start to pay for it.

You can't delete stress. You can build a buffer.

The seven habits below won't make life calm. They help your body bounce back faster after stress, and they protect the two things stress hits hardest: your sleep and your eating.

Step 1

Cortisol has a daily rhythm

It peaks within an hour of waking, then drifts down all day to its lowest point around midnight. The habits below work with that rhythm.

Step 2

On a GLP-1? Stress still needs a plan.

Many people find GLP-1s quiet the constant "food noise." The stress that used to send you to the pantry is still there, though. Give it somewhere else to go.

Pick a non-food stress tool

A short walk, a call to a friend, five slow breaths, a prayer. Decide on it now, before the hard day.

Guard your sleep

Two short nights raised hunger hormones and appetite in a classic study.2 Good sleep makes your medicine's job easier.

Watch the coffee

Caffeine can make nausea and reflux worse. If your stomach is unsettled, try half-caf or tea.

Move most days

Exercise is one of the best stress relievers there is, and it protects muscle while you lose weight.

Step 3

Seven habits to build your buffer

1

Start the day on purpose

Before you check your phone, take two minutes to plan your top tasks, write three things you're grateful for, pray or meditate, or breathe slowly while you picture your day.

2

Get morning light

Step outside within an hour of waking for 5 to 10 minutes. Even one or two minutes helps. Morning light sets your body clock, which helps you sleep that night. Outdoors beats a window, even on a cloudy day.

3

Cut back on caffeine

Keep it to one or two cups and stop by early afternoon. Caffeine's effects last for hours, and coffee six hours before bed cut sleep by about an hour in one study.1 Most adults should stay under 400 mg a day, about four cups of coffee.3

4

Dim the screens at night

Screens in the evening delay melatonin, your sleep hormone, and push your sleep later.4 Put devices down an hour or two before bed, or at least turn on night mode and dim the brightness.

5

Protect your sleep

Aim for 7 to 9 hours, at the same times every day, in a room that's dark, quiet, and cool. Short sleep raises cortisol the next evening.5

6

Write it down at night

Try a brain dump, a gratitude list, or tomorrow's to-do list. In one study, writing a to-do list before bed helped people fall asleep faster.6

7

Use supplements carefully, if at all

Habits come first. See the supplement check below before you buy anything.

Step 4

Cortisol claims, checked

Supported
"Poor sleep raises cortisol."
Yes. After a short night, evening cortisol runs higher.5
Partly true
"High cortisol causes weight gain."
Very high cortisol from a medical condition does. Everyday stress works more through cravings, poor sleep, and less activity.
Partly true
"Caffeine raises cortisol."
It does, but people who drink it every day mostly adapt.7 Its bigger stress cost is usually worse sleep.
Not proven
"Blue light raises cortisol."
Evening screens clearly delay melatonin and sleep.4 A direct cortisol effect hasn't been shown well.
Not proven
"Hours of sleep before midnight count double."
Your deepest sleep comes early in the night, whatever time you fall asleep. A steady schedule matters more than the clock.
Not proven
"Adrenal fatigue" and at-home cortisol tests
A review of 58 studies found no proof that "adrenal fatigue" exists.8 Saliva and urine kits sold online don't guide treatment. If you're worried about your hormones, see your provider.
Step 5

Stress supplements, checked

Supplements aren't checked by the FDA before they're sold. Ask your provider first, especially if you take other medicines, are pregnant, or have thyroid or liver problems.

Partly true
Ashwagandha
Some small trials found lower cortisol and stress scores.9 Rare cases of liver injury have been reported, and Denmark has banned it.10 Avoid it in pregnancy and with thyroid disease.
Partly true
Omega-3 fatty acids
In one trial, fish oil lowered the cortisol spike during a stress test.11 Fatty fish twice a week is a good start.
Partly true
Rhodiola rosea
Small, lower-quality studies suggest it may ease stress-related fatigue. More research is needed.12
Not proven
Magnesium
Helpful if you're low. Proof that it lowers cortisol is weak. Too much causes diarrhea, and people with kidney disease should avoid extra.
Not proven
Vitamin C
Your adrenal glands do use it, but extra vitamin C hasn't been shown to lower stress. Fruits and veggies cover your needs.

See your provider if you notice

  • Fast weight gain in your face, upper back, or belly with wide purple stretch marks, easy bruising, or weak muscles
  • Anxiety or low mood that lasts more than two weeks or gets in the way of daily life
  • Trouble sleeping most nights for a month or more
How this fits your plan

Small steps that carry a lot of weight

Stress care and sleep are two of our eight pillars because they make every other habit easier. Pick one habit from Step 3 and do it every day this week.
